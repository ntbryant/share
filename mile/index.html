The gap

What 46 seconds actually means

Coaches measure fitness with a number called VDOT (from Jack Daniels' running formula). It works like a level score: a 7:46 mile is about a 36, and a 7:00 mile is about a 41. Beginners commonly gain a point every 3–6 weeks when they train consistently.

The science

The mile runs on four engines

The mile is mostly an aerobic race. Lab studies of the 1500 m put the aerobic share at roughly 65–85%, depending on how trained the runner is. The rest is anaerobic power and raw speed. You need all four engines below, but the first two do most of the work.

Engine 1 · Aerobic base

Heart, blood and muscle that use oxygen well

Easy running builds capillaries, mitochondria and a bigger stroke volume. It's slow, boring, and the single biggest lever for a young runner. Most of your running (about 80%) should be easy.

Engine 2 · VO₂max

Your top-end oxygen ceiling

Mile race pace sits right around VO₂max pace. Intervals of 2–4 minutes with recovery jogs (like 400s and 800s) push that ceiling higher. Research on teens suggests this trainability increases around and after the adolescent growth spurt, so ages 14+ are a good time to start structured intervals.

Engine 3 · Lactate threshold

How fast you can go before you "tie up"

Threshold or "tempo" running is comfortably hard: you could say a short sentence, not a paragraph. It raises the pace you can hold before acid builds up quickly, which is what makes lap 3 feel survivable.

Engine 4 · Speed and economy

Less energy per stride

Strides, short fast reps, hill sprints, plyometrics and strength training improve running economy, so goal pace costs less. A 2024 meta-analysis of 31 studies found plyometrics and heavier strength work both improve economy in middle- and long-distance runners.

Why this is especially doable at 14

Boys hit peak height velocity (the fastest part of the growth spurt) around 13.5–14 on average. After that, testosterone rises, muscle and heart size increase, and fitness gains come faster. A 14-year-old who trains well often improves more in a year than an adult would. The flip side: growing bones and tendons are more vulnerable to overuse, so the plan below grows volume slowly and keeps most running easy.

What the research agrees on

  • Consistency beats heroics. Twelve to sixteen weeks of steady training matters more than any single workout.
  • About 80% easy, 20% hard. Elite and youth endurance programs share this pattern. Running hard every day leads to plateaus and injury.
  • Two hard days a week at most. Fitness is built in the recovery between hard sessions.
  • Strength training is safe and helpful for teens when supervised and done with good form (American Academy of Pediatrics, NSCA).

What a teen body needs on top

  • Sleep 8–10 hours. Growth hormone and adaptation happen mostly during sleep.
  • Eat enough. Growing plus training burns a lot. Under-fueling (called RED-S) hurts bones, performance and growth.
  • Carbs before hard days, protein after. A snack with both within an hour of a workout helps recovery.
  • Calcium and vitamin D support bones that are still growing, which matters a lot for runners.

The toolkit

Six workouts that do the job

Paces below are for a 7:46 miler, and they update when you change your times at the top. Every hard workout starts with 10 minutes of easy jogging plus drills and 4 strides, and ends with 5–10 minutes of easy jogging.

Easy

Easy run + strides

25–45 min @ 10:10–11:15 /mi
then 4–6 × 20 s strides

The aerobic base builder. Strides are relaxed, quick 20-second accelerations to about mile pace, with a full walk back. They teach good form at speed without fatigue.

Interval

VO₂max 400s and 800s

6 × 400 @ 2:01, 400 jog
→ 8 × 400 @ 1:56
→ 4 × 800 @ 3:52, 400 jog

The core mile workout. Hard but controlled, and every rep should be the same speed. If the last rep is more than 3 seconds slower, you started too fast.

Repetition

Fast 200s

8 × 200 @ 56 s
200 walk/jog recovery
→ late season @ 51 s

Pure speed and form. Recovery is full on purpose so each rep is fast and relaxed. 52.2 seconds per 200 is 7:00 pace, so these make goal pace feel easy.

Training for a fast mile · Cole Hocker, Olympic 1500 m champion
Power

Hill sprints

6–8 × 10 s up a steep hill
full walk-down recovery

Builds power and stiff, springy tendons with very low injury risk. Add to the end of an easy run once a week in the base phase.

Strength: 2 × 20 minutes a week

Do this after an easy run or on a non-running day: squats or goblet squats, lunges, single-leg calf raises (3 × 15 each leg), glute bridges, planks and side planks. Add light plyometrics: pogo hops, skipping for height, bounding (2–3 sets of 10–20 contacts). Good form first, then add load. This is the cheapest speed you'll ever buy, and it protects knees, shins and Achilles tendons.

Your week

Pick your schedule

More runs means faster progress, but the plan works at every level. Never put two hard days back to back.

The season

16 weeks, four phases

At 3 runs a week, about 16 weeks is a realistic window for this goal (22 weeks at 2 runs, 15 at 3, 13 at 4). Change runs per week above to see the season stretch or shrink. Each phase ends with a check so you know whether you're on track.

WEEKS 1–5

Base

Mostly easy running, strides twice a week, hill sprints, strength. One light tempo session from week 3.

Check: 1-mile time trial → ~7:35
WEEKS 6–10

Build

Add weekly VO₂max intervals (400s → 800s). Tempo every other week. Long run reaches 50 min.

Check: time trial → ~7:20
WEEKS 11–14

Sharpen

Add fast 200s. Intervals move toward goal pace: 400s at 1:48 → 1:44. Keep the easy days easy.

Check: 4 × 400 @ 1:44 with 60 s rest
WEEKS 15–16

Taper + race

Cut volume about 30–40%, keep a little speed (short 200s and strides). Sleep, eat, arrive fresh.

Race: 7:00 or under

The best predictor that you're ready

If you can run 4 × 400 in 1:44 with only 60 seconds of rest between, you have 7:00 fitness. Try it in the middle and at the end of the Sharpen phase. After every time trial, enter your new time at the top of the page.

Race day

Run it even

The most common mile mistake is a fast first lap. Going out 5 seconds too fast can cost 10 or more at the end. Aim for even laps, with lap 3 as your focus lap.

Warm-up (about 25 minutes)

  • 10–12 min easy jog
  • Dynamic drills: leg swings, high knees, butt kicks, A-skips
  • 4 strides building to race pace
  • Finish 5–10 minutes before the start. Stay warm.

The day before

  • 15–20 min easy + 4 strides, or rest
  • Normal meals with extra carbohydrates
  • Race-day meal 2–3 hours before; light snack 1 hour before
  • No new shoes, foods or workouts
Guardrails

Stay healthy, keep improving

Injury is what usually stops young runners, not lack of talent. Use these traffic-light rules.

Green: train

General muscle soreness that fades during warm-up. Tiredness after hard days. Legs feel heavy the morning after intervals.

Yellow: easy day or rest

Pain that changes how you run, lingers into the next day, or is in one spot. Swap the workout for an easy day or cross-training (bike, swim).

Red: stop and see a doctor

Pain in one spot on a bone (possible stress fracture), pain at the knee bump below the kneecap or at the heel (growth plate irritation), or any pain that gets worse as you run.

  • Grow slowly. Increase weekly running time by no more than about 10%, and take an easier week every 4th week.
  • Shoes: replace around 300–500 miles. Rotate two pairs if you can.
  • During growth spurts (you can feel it as sore knees or heels, or suddenly tight hamstrings), back off volume for a week or two.
  • Other sports count. Soccer or basketball practice is training load too. Count it in the week.
